1.	Literature Review (Completed)
1.1.	Introduction
Natural disasters represent a persistent and evolving challenge to the safety and resilience of the built environment. Among these hazards, earthquakes and tsunamis are particularly catastrophic due to the combination of extreme seismic and hydrodynamic forces imposed onto structural systems and infrastructure (Satake, 2014). Historical events, such as the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake-tsunami and 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, have demonstrated critical structural failures, extensive loss of life and vast socio-economic disruption (Paulay and Priestley, 1992; EEFIT, 2006).
Effective disaster resilience requires the integration of structural engineering principles with architectural mechanisms rather than isolated technical solutions. While structural systems govern the design’s capacity to resist, dissipate and redistribute seismic and hydrodynamic forces, architectural configurations equally influence how structures perform under dynamic loading (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007). However, the technical sophistication of these mechanisms individually does not determine their overall effectiveness. The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) (2019) emphasises the need for effective communication for increased public understanding and engagement. These are fundamental components of disaster resilience, particularly in disaster-prone regions, where misinterpretation of engineering mechanisms can inadvertently increase vulnerability. 
‘Disaster to Engagement’ reframes structural engineering and architecture as disciplines that are not only technical but also inherently communicative. Complex engineering mechanisms and architectural resilience principles must be translated into tangible learning experiences. This approach supports comprehensive learning through demonstrations that enable audiences to visualise intuitive relationships and varying system behaviours. 
By examining earthquake and tsunami mechanisms, structural and architectural resilience principles and effective engagement approaches, this literature review establishes the theoretical foundation for the development of an interactive application that demonstrates the role of engineering strategies in natural disaster risk mitigation.
1.2.	Understanding Natural Disasters
1.2.1.	Overview of Earthquakes and Tsunamis
Earthquakes and tsunamis are among the most destructive natural hazards, generated primarily by tectonic plate interactions (Satake, 2014). Classified as geohazards, these events represent the culmination of seismic and hydrodynamic processes that can lead to catastrophic societal impacts (Paulay and Priestley, 1992; EEFIT, 2006). Historically, these events have caused widespread destruction, significant loss of life and long-term socio-economic disruption, with consequences that extend beyond the immediate disaster zone, influencing national stability and political dynamics (Aksoy et al., 2025; Ishigaki et al., 2013). Illustrated in Figure 1, the UNDRR indicate that the highest economic losses were caused by major earthquakes and tsunamis, specifically the 1995 Kobe and 2011 Tōhoku earthquakes, highlighting the disproportionate impact that these events have on global economies.  (UNDRR , 2016)
 
Although earthquakes and tsunamis are driven by different physical processes, many of their underlying mechanisms are interconnected, with large undersea earthquakes commonly acting as the primary trigger for tsunamis (EEFIT, 2006). In extreme cases, rapid vertical displacement can cause earthquake-tsunamis [FM1.1]where abnormally large tsunami waves are produced despite misalignment with their seismic magnitude (Synolakis and Kong, 2006). Data from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) reinforce this relationship, highlighting the vast majority of tsunami events being triggered from seismic activity (Figure 2). (NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration), 2017)(Schellart et al., 2011)
 
Rising demographic and spatial trends highlight the increasing global vulnerability to these geohazards. Earthquakes and tsunamis account for a considerable proportion of global disaster-related mortality. According to Neumann et al. (2015), nearly 3.6 million people currently live within 150km of coastlines, with projections suggesting that over 1 billion people are expected to reside in low-lying coastal areas by 2050, further intensifying the potential exposure to future disasters (IPCC, 2019).[FM2.1] Additionally, rapid urban expansion in Asia and the Pacific is heightening the vulnerability of emerging megacities, many of which are developing directly within seismically active and coastal hazard zones (UN-Habitat, 2020; UNDRR, 2019). 
1.2.2.	Earthquake Mechanisms and Structural Implications  
Earthquakes occur when accumulated strain within the Earth’s lithosphere is suddenly released due to tectonic plate movement at subduction, transformation or collision boundaries (Satake, 2014). This abrupt expulsion of energy produces seismic waves that propagate through the ground, generating complex vibration patterns. The global distribution of these tectonic settings is displayed in Figure 3. 
 
Deeper subduction zones, which accumulate increased concentrations of strain, represent the most dangerous seismic environments (EEFIT, 2006). These zones create the largest seismic events, including magnitude 9.0 megathrust earthquakes such as the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake, which exhibit extreme ground shaking and tsunamis. Crustal earthquakes typically form along transform boundaries, where tectonic plates slide laterally past one another (Satake, 2014). Their shallow depth and proximity to the Earth’s surface result in more intense and focused shaking. Historically, the 1995 Kobe earthquake produced a magnitude of 6.9, significantly lower than Tōhoku, but ruptured directly beneath the city, resulting in disproportionate destruction (Ghosh, 1995).
Within structural engineering, seismic wave behaviour governs the transmission of earthquake-induced energy through the earth’s crust and into the built environment (Paulay and Priestley, 1992). Seismic waves are categorised into body waves and surface waves. Body waves originate from the rupture point of the hypocentre and propagate through the Earth’s interior, whereas surface waves travel along the ground surface. Body waves comprise of Primary (P) Waves and Secondary (S) waves, where P-waves induce compressional motion and typically less damage. However, slower S-waves generate horizontal shear deformations and are therefore more responsible for structural failures (Satake, 2014). Furthermore, surface waves, which are generated from the interaction and conversion of body waves at the earth’s surface, produce prolonged and high-amplitude ground motions. This means that they can trap and channel extreme seismic energy along shallower geological layers, leading to more destructive effects (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007).
According to Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky (2007), structural implications from earthquakes depend on a system’s ability to absorb, dissipate, and redistribute the dynamic seismic loads imposed upon it. Modern seismic design philosophy focuses on inelastic structural response and resilience-based engineering. When lateral demands exceed design capacity, a range of failure mechanisms can develop. Soft-storey collapse, typically occurring in buildings with open-plan ground floors, was especially observed in Kobe (Ghosh, 1995). Furthermore, resonance poses as another failure mode experienced by structures. Chopra (2017) refers to resonance as the condition in which the dominant frequency of ground motion during an earthquake coincides with the natural frequency of a structure. This results in a significant amplification of the seismic wave response and deformation. 
Another hazard in seismic environments is liquefaction, which occurs when saturated and loose soil loses general strength and stiffness under cyclic loading (Chopra, 2017). This process temporarily reduces the soil’s capacity to support structural loads, causing buildings to tilt or undergo significant differential movement without necessarily experiencing structural failure. Geotechnical elements such as stiffness, depth, and soil composition influence how seismic forces alter the distribution of inertial forces and therefore, their transmission into a structural system. Liquefaction was widely documented during both the 2011 Tōhoku and 1995 Kobe earthquakes, where its effects were most severe in areas constructed on reclaimed land or soft alluvial soils. These ground conditions significantly increase susceptibility to liquefaction, highlighting the critical influence of local soil characteristics on seismic vulnerability (Bruneau, 1998).
Collectively, these mechanisms illustrate the multifaceted nature of earthquake loading, where ground motion and soil characteristics, alongside structural design configuration, interact to influence overall structural performance. The complexity of seismic behaviour highlights the difficulty in conceptualising the interactions, hence providing a critical foundation for examining design resilience in structural and architectural systems. 
1.2.3.	Tsunami Mechanisms and Structural Implications
Tsunamis are extreme-wavelength ocean waves that are generated by the sudden displacement of large water masses, commonly caused by undersea earthquakes in subduction zones. When a megathrust rupture produces rapid vertical displacement of the seafloor, the immediate water column experiences uplift. This initiates a series of destructive waves that propagate across the ocean basin (EEFIT, 2006). Although earthquakes remain the fundamental mechanism behind tsunamis, Satake (2014) notes that the event can also be caused by volcanic eruptions, underwater landslides and faulting deformation. 
Tsunami waves behave differently to normal wind-driven surface waves. The Earthquake Engineering Field Investigation Team (EEFIT) (2006) note that within deep water columns, tsunami waves travel at speeds of up to 500-900 km/h. Despite this, their energy only becomes evident upon reaching shallow coastal waters, where the shoaling effect causes wave velocity to decrease while significantly amplifying wave height, transforming barely perceptible offshore waves into destructive walls of water (Synolakis and Kong, 2006). Recorded run-up heights exceeded 40 metres [FM3.1]in regions exposed during the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake-tsunami, highlighting the extreme vertical reach of tsunami inundation, which is highly sensitive to coastal geometry, slope and frictional characteristics (Mori et al., 2011; EERI, 2011). The severity of tsunami inundation during this event is reflected in the associated human impacts recorded across affected regions, as summarised in Figure 4. (Ishigaki et al., 2013)
 
Tsunamis generate a series of dynamic and hydrostatic forces upon impact with coastal infrastructure. Hydrodynamic loads, which are generated by high-velocity flow impacting building interfaces, exert intensive drag and impulsive forces capable of breaching and ultimately failing walls (EEFIT, 2006). Additionally, hydrostatic pressure differentials develop from standing water columns that are trapped within buildings after impact. General hazards also develop from debris impact forces, which become high-energy projectiles within the highly turbulent flow (EERI, 2011). Furthermore, high-velocity water movement can scour supporting soil around shallow foundations, undermining structural columns and footings. This mechanism closely mirrors liquefaction, compromising foundation support and increasing the likelihood of structural settlement or collapse (Chopra, 2017). Non-engineered structures, particularly masonry and timber buildings, are especially vulnerable due to their limited ductility and weak lateral capacity.
Structural behaviour during tsunami inundation entails multi-dimensional processes, such as wave transformation, debris interaction, and progressive collapse. These determine how structural systems respond to catastrophic events. Understanding the mechanisms is essential for informing the engineering and architectural strategies required to mitigate its effects. By identifying how hydrodynamic forces and impact loads affect built form, engineers are better positioned to design resilient structural and architectural systems. [FM4.1]
1.3.	Structural and Architectural Resilience Principles
1.3.1.	Engineering Strategies for Earthquakes
Earthquake-resilient design relies on the integration of structural engineering mechanisms with architectural considerations, aimed at limiting damage and preventing collapse under seismic loading. These strategies manage the complex seismic demands through absorption, dissipation, and redistribution, proving fundamental to effective seismic mitigation.
1.3.1.1.	Absorption and Resistance
Against seismic forces, a structure's initial mitigation is its inherent strength and stiffness. These qualities govern a design’s ability to resist and limit lateral deformation and maintain stability during extensive ground shaking (Paulay and Priestley, 1992). Strength defines the maximum internal force a structural element can sustain before failure, while stiffness controls how much deformation occurs under a given lateral load (Chopra, 2017). Historically, seismic loads have been idealised as mass-proportional lateral forces that are resisted elastically, with displacement limited by the structure’s elastic stiffness. Stiffness remains a fundamental parameter, as it directly governs drift, accelerations, and the distributions of seismic forces throughout the entire structural system (Paulay and Priestley, 1992).
To enhance stiffness and redistribute inertia forces, mitigation systems such as shear walls and braced and moment-resisting frames are commonly employed. Shear walls act as vertical cantilevers capable of reducing design sway and resisting in-plane shear and therefore enhances resistance to bending and shear. When placed symmetrically and continuously, they reduce storey drift and provide robust lateral resistance (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007). Braced frames achieve lateral strength through triangulated geometry, which promotes efficient force distribution paths and stiffness while actively limiting demand on surrounding structural elements. 
Specifically in high-rise buildings, where seismic demand is amplified by height-related flexibility and dynamic effects, extensive reinforcement mechanisms can be supplemented. Systems such as outrigger trusses, belt walls, and mega-braced frames couple the building core to perimeter columns. Chadalawada (2015) notes that these systems work synchronously with core wall structures providing uniform stiffness distribution across the building height, amplifying design robustness. Furthermore, multi-dimensional base isolation systems are capable of reducing the transmission of ground accelerations to the superstructure by lengthening the building’s fundamental period and filtering out high-frequency components of ground motion. A comparative analysis is presented in Figure 5, where results indicate that base isolation is the superior technique for absorbing seismic energy (Chadalawada, 2015).
 
1.3.1.2.	Dissipation: Ductility and Energy Absorption
A fundamental principle of earthquake-resistant design is the controlled dissipation of seismic energy through ductile inelastic behaviour. Chopra (2017) defines elastic behaviour as a linear relationship between force and deformation, which results in no permanent damage. However, designing structures to remain entirely elastic under earthquakes requires unrealistically high strength and stiffness. This results in structures that are neither practical nor economical to build (Paulay and Priestley, 1992).
Therefore, engineers must ensure that inelastic deformations occur in a predictable and non-brittle manner. Controlled inelasticity enables the structure to undergo large cyclic deformations while maintaining residual strength and stiffness, preventing shear failures and connection fractures. Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky (2007) notes that this internal mechanism concentrates damage into specifically designed ductile components, such as plastic hinges in beams, which enables the superstructure to absorb and dissipate extensive amounts of seismic energy without total collapse. Ultimately, modern seismic design accepts that structures will deform beyond their elastic limits during strong shaking, relying on controlled ductile behaviour to dissipate energy efficiently (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007).
Fardis (2009) refers to ductility as a structure’s capacity to undergo large deformations while sustaining a substantial portion of its initial strength. Through ductility, structural elements can repeatedly deform under cyclic loading, dissipating seismic energy via hysteretic behaviour. This behaviour refers to nonlinear force-deformation of a structural element that forms closed hysteresis loops (Chopra, 2017). Illustrated in Figure 6, the area enclosed within the hysteresis loop represents the energy dissipated by a structure, therefore reducing seismic demand on the superstructure (Chopra, 2017). (Colombo and Negro, 2005)
 
Furthermore, ductility is not uniformly distributed throughout all structural systems. Commonly used structural mitigation systems exhibit ductility in various concentrations. Moment-resisting frames can be utilised to provide ductile lateral resistance through its application of plastic beam-column joints (Chopra, 2017). Shear walls provide limited ductility unless purposely detailed with boundary elements and confinement reinforcement (Paulay and Priestley, 1992). Additionally, buckling-restrained braces provide highly ductile and symmetric hysteresis loops, while traditional concentric braced frames may experience strength degradation due to brace buckling  (Sabelli, Mahin and Chang, 2003; Chopra, 2017). Furthermore, effective damping can also be achieved through base-isolated structures, where isolator deformation exhibits varying dissipation capacities. Kelly (1990) finds that this method increases damping exceeding 20-30% and therefore significantly reduces the overall demands on the superstructure.
Similarly, seismic damping devices provide supplemental mechanisms for reducing structural acceleration and energy absorption (Chopra, 2017).
Common damping systems include:
•	Viscous Dampers
o	Exert forces proportional to relative velocity, providing effective control of high-frequency components (Soong and Dargush, 1997).
•	Friction Dampers
o	Dissipates energy through sliding at controlled friction interfaces and is effective in low to mid-rise structures (Pall and Marsh, 1982).
•	Tuned Mass Dampers
o	Frequently used in high-rise structures to reduce peak accelerations by engaging mass inertia (Connor and Laflamme, 2014).
Collectively, these mechanisms significantly reduce seismic energy demand throughout the superstructure, enabling improved performance under varying ground motions.
Additionally, Direct Displacement-Based Design is a metric that forms ductility and damping as fundamental parameters, where inelastic responses are modelled using Equivalent Viscous Damping to quantify hysteretic energy dissipated by the system (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007). This allows seismic demand to be expressed in terms of displacement and deformation capacity, which enables designers to align design principles more closely with actual structural behaviour under strong ground conditions. 
1.3.1.3.	Redistribution: Capacity Design and Load Path Control
Capacity design philosophy serves as a fundamental principle in modern seismic engineering, ensuring that critical structural elements are protected from brittle failures. Due to the fluctuating and spatially variable seismic concentrations induced to designs, the structure must be capable of redistributing internal force demands away from vulnerable areas within the structure, and towards intentionally detailed elements that deform inelastically (Paulay and Priestley, 1992). This inelastic action underpins the engineering development of controlled plastic mechanisms that preserve the building’s gravity-load capacity, preventing catastrophic collapse.
There is an established hierarchy within capacity design between ductile elements and capacity-protected elements utilised in structural systems. Ductile components, such as dissipative fuses and plastic walls, are intentionally designed to yield first under cyclic loading, while columns and shear-critical walls remain predominantly elastic (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007). This mechanism governs the system’s ability to control inelastic deformation by avoiding unintended failure modes, such as shear cracking and compression buckling. Fardis (2009) writes that this distinction between ductile and non-ductile regions remains vital for maintaining global stability even under severe cyclic loading.
Capacity design can be initialised through internal systems such as weak-beam/strong-column mechanisms in moment-resisting frames. Eurocode 8 (2004) formalises this requirement to prevent collapse in soft-storey buildings. A strength ratio interface between beams and columns ensures that under cyclic seismic forces, beam plastic hinges develop before column yielding. The formation of plastic elements, displayed in Figure 7, prevents brittle collapse, ensures that lateral load paths are persistently stable and vertical load bearing capacity is constant even during significant inelastic rotations (Paulay and Priestley, 1992). 1.3.2.	Engineering Strategies for Tsunamis
Tsunami-resilient design requires distinct engineering mechanisms from those utilised for seismic protection. Tsunamis subject structures to hydrodynamic forces, hydrostatic pressures, debris impact and severe foundation scour, often exceeding typical lateral design loads (EEFIT, 2006). The duration and spatial variability of these forces necessitate specialised structural and architectural systems to minimise vulnerability in coastal environments. Understanding these mechanisms establishes the basis for evaluating how engineering interventions can mitigate damage caused by tsunamis. 
1.3.2.1.	Structural Systems and Resilience-Oriented Design
Reinforced Concrete (RC) has consistently displayed superior performance in major tsunami events due to its inherent stiffness, ductility, and mass. These characteristics enable RC systems to resist the intense hydrodynamic and debris impact forces generated by inundation flows. Post-disaster assessments following the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami and the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami frequently documented that well-detailed RC moment-resisting frames remained structurally intact even under inundation depths exceeding 5 metres (Suppasri et al., 2013; EEFIT, 2006), Collapse in these cases was attributed to insufficient construction quality and inadequate joint detailing, leading to brittle failures such as joint shear cracking and localised punching. The combination of these failure modes compromised the structure’s gravity-load path once hydrodynamic forces exceeded local capacity, highlighting the importance of robust detailing and material quality in tsunami-prone regions (EERI, 2011). RC systems possess significant structural resilience against tsunami-induced forces and can effectively maintain stability under extreme lateral and uplift pressures, providing essential life safety during disaster events.
Additionally, foundation behaviour provides a critical mechanism in determining the survivability of buildings to tsunami inundation. Structures supported on shallow footings are highly vulnerable to scour, erosion and building overturning, especially when exposed to the high velocity flows from powerful tsunami currents. Contrastingly, deep pile foundations significantly outperform shallow foundation systems through the transfer of structural loads into deep and more stable soil layers. The EEFIT (2006) notes that these deeper strata are less susceptible to failure mechanisms such as erosion and liquefaction. Additionally, stilted foundation structures, commonly utilised in regions affected by the 2004 Indian Ocean and 2011 Tōhoku earthquakes, have showcased favourable performance. An open-plan substructure allowed inundation flows to pass through the building, negating the impact of increased hydrodynamic pressures against solid walls and therefore limiting structural damage (Suppasri et al., 2013). 
1.3.2.2.	Infrastructure Protection, Modelling and Early Warning Systems
Damage and impacts from tsunamis extend beyond isolated structural design. Infrastructure such as transportation networks, electricity and water supply systems are especially vulnerable to inundation depth and geomorphology (Løvholt et al., 2014). These systems remain critical as they support emergency response and evacuation, exacerbating post-disaster vulnerability and exemplifying the importance of structural resilience in infrastructure design. 
During the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, a series of failure mechanisms severely disrupted post-disaster mobility, such as scouring of weak base materials and bridge foundations, increased buoyancy leading to bridge superstructure uplift and the significant loss of lateral restraint (FEMA P-55, 2011)[FM5.1]. Similarly, the EERI (2011) found multiple coastal bridges that were exposed to deck displacement and foundation erosion during the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami. Therefore, rapid post-tsunami connectivity is imperative for restoring essential services and supporting effective recovery. This can be achieved by establishing interim transport links such as Bailey Bridges, which function as modular, temporary bridge systems capable of rapidly reinstating severed infrastructure corridors (Williams et al., 2021). These systems provide an efficient and practical solution for overcoming scoured bridge sites and washed-out channels that remain particularly effective in tsunami-affected regions where debris accumulation and fragile soil conditions limit conventional reconstruction methods.
Robust engineering mitigation strategies are integrated with advanced modelling and monitoring frameworks, harnessing early-warning technologies that support efficient public response to tsunami risk management. Advancements in tsunami detection technologies and geospatial analysis have improved forecasting of tsunami arrival times, inundation depths, and impact zones (Løvholt et al., 2014). A key technological component of modern tsunami detection is the Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis system (DART). Developed by NOAA, DART buoys operate as global seabed-mounted bottom pressure recorders capable of detecting minute changes in the water column associated with tsunami wave propagation. González et al.(1998) note that these sensors can identify tsunami signals in deep-ocean conditions, transmitting real-time data via acoustic and satellite links to regional tsunami warning centres (Figure 8). (NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration), 2017)
 
Additionally, numerical tsunami modelling further enhances the effectiveness of early warning systems. High-resolution numerical methods, calibrated using real-time offshore observations and post-disaster data, have improved the accuracy in prediction relating to inundation depth and tsunami parameter predictions (Sugawara, 2021). These advancements in digital modelling systems enable informed hazard assessment for nearshore engineering processes such as bore formation and coastal topography interaction. Collectively, the integration of monitoring technologies and numerical modelling provides a robust framework for representative evacuation decisions, infrastructure protection strategies and ultimately, tsunami risk management.
1.3.2.3.	Urban Planning, Coastal Management and Nature-Based Mitigation
Tsunami risk mitigation can be further achieved through strategic urban planning and coastal management policies. These mechanisms aim to reduce disaster exposure on a regional scale by limiting the concentration of people and assets within high-hazard coastal zones. By reducing exposure, the potential socio-economic consequences of extreme tsunami events can be significantly mitigated. 
Following the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake-tsunami, restricted development areas along exposed coastlines were introduced to prevent reconstruction and limit future exposure to extreme tsunami hazards (Cho, 2014; EEFIT, 2006). These exclusion zones were informed by post-event inundation mapping and aimed to reduce future loss of life through relocating critical infrastructure away from high-risk areas. However, Cho (2014) highlights that the effectiveness of these mechanisms depends on governance capacity and socio-economic considerations, particularly where relocation disrupts livelihoods and coastal communities.
Additionally, nature-based protection has been employed as a supplementary mitigation strategy, where coral reefs and sand dunes are utilised to reduce tsunami wave energy and dissipate flow velocities before inundation reaches the built environment (UNEP, 2005). Following the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, Danielsen et al. (2005) observed that coastal areas shielded by mangrove forests and coral reefs generally experienced reduced structural damage and flow velocities compared to exposed coastlines. These natural barriers contributed to tsunami-wave energy dissipation through increased hydraulic roughness and wave attenuation, therefore reducing overall hydrodynamic force intensity. Despite this, Danielsen et al. (2005) additionally highlight how these processes, as standalone defences, are insufficient against extreme tsunami events and must therefore be integrated with synthetic mitigation strategies to achieve reliable disaster risk reduction.
1.3.3.	Architectural Design Principles
Architectural design plays a fundamental role in determining how structural systems respond to dynamic earthquake and tsunami loading. Design considerations related to spatial configuration and building orientation directly govern load path direction, deformation patterns, and internal occupant movement during disaster events. 
Beyond structural response, architectural design can determine human response and vulnerability during rapid-onset disasters. Clear spatial configuration, intuitive circulation layouts and accessible vertical evacuation routes can enhance urban legibility and therefore support faster and safer evacuation (FEMA P-646[FM6.1], 2019).
In seismic-resistant design, plan regularity and symmetry significantly influence structural response. Buildings with continuous vertical elements and regular forms promote predictable load transfer, efficient energy dissipation and controlled inelastic behaviour, enhancing overall seismic performance (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007). Continuous architectural form facilitates effective redistribution of seismic forces, supporting capacity design principles and plastic mechanism formation. Conversely, irregularities in architectural configuration, such as discontinuous structural walls and soft-storey configurations, introduce undesirable torsional demands and stress concentrations, amplifying seismic damage to the superstructure. Ghosh (1995) observed disproportionate damage to soft-storey ground floor structures during the 1995 Kobe earthquake, where uneven mass and stiffness distribution induced twisting motions, concentrating load demands into unintended brittle components, ultimately resulting in structural failure (Priestley, Calvi and Kowalsky, 2007). 
In tsunami-resistant design, structural resilience can also be influenced by architectural configuration. Building orientation and spatial regularity affect how inundation flows interact with the superstructure. Structures incorporating elevated floors or permeable lower storeys allow tsunami flows to pass through the building, rather than accumulating against rigid facades, reducing hydrostatic pressures and overturning demands that otherwise lead to structural failure (EEFIT, 2006). Robust vertical load-bearing systems are capable of improving building stability by limiting force accumulation, enabling controlled structural damage without total collapse (EERI, 2011). Furthermore, strategic wall orientation perpendicular to the coastline improves a structure's resistance to lateral water pressure, contributing to increased structural stability. In contrast, shear and masonry infill walls aligned parallel to inundation flow may fail to relieve internal pressure deficits, preventing excessive force transfer to primary structure elements (EEFIT, 2006). 
Collectively, architectural principles equally contribute to overall superstructure resilience performance as structural mechanisms, rather than serving solely as aesthetic considerations. This integration of structural engineering and architectural design principles is essential for mitigating both structural damage and human vulnerability during earthquakes and tsunamis.
1.4.	Engineering Outreach and Engagement[FM7.1]
Engineering plays a critical role in determining the safety and resilience of the built environment in the context of natural hazards. Although the technical performance of engineering mechanisms is fundamental to maintaining this level, public understanding and appreciation additionally influence disaster responses.  In disaster-prone regions, misinterpretation of engineering mitigation strategies can undermine the mechanisms’ intended benefits. Ultimately, this highlights the growing requirement for effective public engagement within the engineering industry. 
The UNDRR emphasises that reducing disaster losses cannot be achieved solely through robust infrastructure but also involves informing communities' understanding of associated risk and appropriate responses to warnings and protective measures (UNDRR, 2019). The IPCC conceptualises risk as an interaction between hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. This relationship is displayed in Figure 9, which highlights that disaster impacts are amplified when high-magnitude hazards coincide with increased human exposure and systemic vulnerability (IPCC, 2022). While the frequency and magnitude of earthquakes and tsunamis are largely uncontrollable, exposure and vulnerability are significantly influenced by population distribution and urban development. As coastal populations continue to grow and urbanisation expands into seismically active regions, reducing vulnerability through effective engineering mitigation and public understanding becomes increasingly fundamental. (Neumann et al., 2015; UNDRR, 2019)
 
A persistent challenge presents the technical complexity of engineering concepts, which makes it difficult for non-specialist audiences to interpret. For low-probability, high-impact events such as earthquakes and tsunamis, it is imperative that technical information is effectively communicated. Misinterpretation may increase vulnerability and can lead to disengagement and false perceptions of safety (Burns, O’Connor and Stocklmayer, 2003). Consequently, complex technical principles must be translated into accessible and meaningful narratives. Effective communication of these mechanisms can lead to increased engagement and better public appreciation for engineering strategies (Illingworth and Allen, 2016).
Engineering engagement can therefore be harnessed as a mechanism for enhancing societal resilience through improving public understanding of how engineering strategies can mitigate natural hazards. Ultimately, this highlights the importance of clear engagement tools capable of translating complex technical principles into tangible learning experiences that support disaster preparedness and risk awareness.
1.5.	Interactive Demonstrations in Engineering Education
Interactive demonstrations serve as an effective mechanism for establishing clear engineering concepts through their significant pedagogical value in engineering education. Prince (2004)  notes that visual and active learning methods can improve conceptual understanding and engagement compared to passive instructional methods. Within engineering contexts, demonstrations enable audiences to observe cause-and-effect relationships and explore complex system behaviour under varying conditions.  This fosters an intuitive understanding of dynamic processes that are otherwise difficult to conceptualise and teach. 
This pedagogical value is crucial for hazard-related engineering, where disaster phenomena such as structural response and failure mechanisms occur rapidly and cannot be easily conveyed through static diagrams. Well-curated interactive demonstrations serve as cognitive bridges between theory and real-world applications, supporting deeper public understanding of engineering mitigation strategies (Kolb, 2014). Therefore, interactive tools offer effective mechanisms for communicating engineering resilience to both specialist and non-specialist audiences equally.
1.6.	Conclusion
In conclusion, the findings directly inform the development of an interactive application capable of bridging the gap between technical knowledge and public understanding. Earthquakes and tsunamis impose dynamic and severe impacts on the built environment through a range of different mechanisms. Earthquake loading is governed by seismic wave propagation, local soil conditions, and structural configuration, while tsunamis subject buildings and infrastructure to intense hydrodynamic forces, debris impact, and foundation scour. Although these mechanisms differ in nature, they are inherently interconnected, highlighting the need for integrated structural and architectural design considerations when addressing natural hazard resilience. 
Despite their distinct attributes, effective mitigation strategies across both disasters are underpinned by core structural engineering resilience principles including adequate strength and stiffness, controlled ductility and energy dissipation, and capacity-based load redistribution. Tsunami-specific strategies such as RC systems, elevated structural forms and robust foundation solutions demonstrate significant improvements in superstructure resilience, especially when integrated with early-warning systems and infrastructure protection strategies. In parallel, purposeful urban planning and nature-based mitigation can further reduce exposure to inundation hazards before they reach the built environment.
Architectural configuration additionally plays a critical role in influencing structural response and overall system resilience. Regular building forms, continuous load paths, and clear spatial configuration support predictable structural behaviour and safer human response during disaster events. This integration of structural engineering with architectural design significantly enhances superstructure performance to disaster resilience while actively increasing human safety. 
Furthermore, public communication and engagement are essential components of disaster resilience. For low-probability, high-impact events such as earthquakes and tsunamis, interactive applications offer compelling pedagogical value by enabling users to intuitively explore how engineering strategies modify structural and architectural performance under differing cyclic loading mechanisms. Translating complex resilience principles into tangible learning experiences has the potential to enhance societal awareness of disaster preparedness and strengthen public engagement with engineering involvement in natural disasters.
